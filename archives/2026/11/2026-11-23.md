### [SUAF: Spectral Universal Advantage Features for Zero-Shot Policy Improvement](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27919)

- **Signal Score:** `9.2/10.0`
- **Links:** [arXiv Page](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27919) | [Direct PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.27919v1)

**TL;DR:** Reward-independent representations enable reinforcement learning agents to reuse knowledge across tasks, but limited representation capacity raises three fundam...

<details>
<summary><b>Abstract & Metadata</b></summary>

Reward-independent representations enable reinforcement learning agents to reuse knowledge across tasks, but limited representation capacity raises three fundamental questions: what statistical dependence should these representations capture, which components of that dependence should be retained for policy improvement, and how does discarding the remaining components affect decision-making? We address these questions through canonical dependence analysis. For a reference policy, we construct an action-centered, positive-lag canonical dependence matrix (CDM) that isolates the additional predictive contribution of the current action beyond the current state. Its singular value decomposition yields Spectral Universal Advantage Features (SUAF), paired features that recover delayed advantage through linear reward projections. We show that the spectrum quantifies mutual information between current action and future state-action visitation conditioned on current state and determines reward specific approximation errors. Retaining the leading modes gives optimal rank-constrained approximations under both isotropic average-case and reward-uniform minimax criteria. Separating the immediate reward contrast also avoids the spectral floor arising from direct compression of the full advantage operator. To learn SUAF from reward-free data, we develop an action-centered Soft-HGR objective with bidirectional temporal-difference estimation. We further connect spectral approximation errors to sufficient conditions for policy improvement. Finite-state experiments validate the spectral predictions and feature-learning method, while zero-shot evaluations on discrete tasks and continuous-control benchmarks demonstrate the utility of the learned representations across rewards.

<summary><b>Abstract & Metadata</b></summary>

Vision-language models (VLMs) exhibit strong semantic understanding but remain limited in their ability to reason about metric 3D space in the physical world. Existing VLMs typically infer 3D scene structure from one or more images without known camera calibration or cross-view geometric constraints. This makes metric 3D inference inherently ambiguous and leaves it unclear whether their spatial predictions are grounded in visual evidence or driven primarily by semantic priors. In contrast, calibrated stereo provides explicit cross-view geometry that resolves this ambiguity and offers a more reliable foundation for metric 3D perception and geometric generalization. We investigate how VLMs can learn transferable 3D representations directly from calibrated binocular observations. To this end, we introduce StereoVLM, the first geometry-aware binocular VLM for metric 3D spatial reasoning. StereoVLM follows a hierarchical learning paradigm spanning from cross-view correspondence and disparity estimation to calibration-conditioned metric depth perception and semantic spatial reasoning. We further find that disparity supervision generalizes substantially better than direct binocular depth supervision on unseen environments. Intervention results further indicate that the model's performance on Stereo3D-Bench benefits from valid cross-view geometry and cannot be explained solely by additional visual tokens or semantic priors. Hierarchical geometric supervision also yields average gains of 3.54 points across five spatial benchmarks and 4.3 percentage points in RoboCasa manipulation success.

### [QuantStream3D: Geometry-Aligned Low-Bit KV Cache Quantization for Streaming Visual Geometry Transformers](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27901)

- **Authors:** Zunhai Su, Keyu Fan, Ruishan Guo, Zhongzhu Zhou et al. (+6)

- **Links:** [arXiv Page](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27901) | [Direct PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.27901v1)

**TL;DR:** we propose QuantStream3D, to our knowledge the first dedicated KV quantization framework for streaming visual geometry transformers.

<details>
<summary><b>Abstract & Metadata</b></summary>

Streaming visual geometry transformers retain historical key-value (KV) states as cross-view geometric memory, but growing caches constrain long-sequence inference. We systematically characterize KV quantization in streaming geometry and uncover two linked challenges: Nonuniform Geometric Sensitivity and Cache-Geometry Mismatch. Quantization sensitivity varies across precision choices and cache locations, while perturbed retrieval changes geometry-head inputs and propagates through subsequent predictions and KV writes. Guided by these findings, we propose QuantStream3D, to our knowledge the first dedicated KV quantization framework for streaming visual geometry transformers. Geometry-Guided Mixed-Precision KV Quantization protects sensitive cross-view evidence, while Geometry-Aligned Quantization-Aware Distillation jointly adapts transformer blocks and geometry heads on student-generated quantized history. Across five benchmarks and two backbones, QuantStream3D achieves near-lossless geometric accuracy in most settings with predominantly 2-bit KV caches, and many configurations surpass unquantized inference. Compared with LLM-oriented KV quantizers, it reduces TUM absolute trajectory error and NeuralRGBD completeness error by up to 57.4% and 69.7%, respectively. Integration with existing native cache-pruning methods further pushes compression while improving reconstruction quality on NeuralRGBD. Our optimized StreamVGGT backend achieves $5.05\times$ physical KV compression and 23.1% lower latency at batch size one. Under a fixed memory budget on the same GPU, it supports $5.4\times$ the maximum batch size and up to $1.67\times$ the peak throughput of unquantized inference. Code and pretrained checkpoints will be made publicly available.

### [MemOnce: A Scalable Memory Architecture for Large Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27973)
- **Category:** 🧠 LLMs & Reasoning (`llm_reasoning`)
- **Authors:** Chang Nie, Chaoyou Fu, Songlin Liu, Yiqing Hu et al. (+1)
- **Signal Score:** `9.1/10.0`
- **Links:** [arXiv Page](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27973) | [Direct PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.27973v1)

**TL;DR:** We introduce MemOnce, a foundation architecture that learns scalable memory states for language models.

<details>
<summary><b>Abstract & Metadata</b></summary>

Deploying large language models (LLMs) in long-horizon and continual interactions requires maintaining reusable memory states for future inference, posing challenges for existing architectures. Standard Transformers accumulate layer-wise, token-level KV caches, incurring linear GPU memory growth and quadratic computational complexity as the context expands. In contrast, linear-time architectures compress history into fixed-size states, but struggle to retain fine-grained details. We introduce MemOnce, a foundation architecture that learns scalable memory states for language models. MemOnce is based on two core designs: (a) Memory encoder-decoder: it caches only a small subset of final-layer hidden states as compact memory representations and dynamically decodes them back into KV caches for attention layers; (b) Memory sparse attention: for each continual input, MemOnce adaptively retrieves relevant memory representations, with the sparse indices computed only once across layers. Coupling these two designs enables efficient on-the-fly memory loading and KV cache decoding, drastically reducing the GPU memory footprint and achieving linear-time complexity. We conduct extensive experiments to show that MemOnce outperforms Transformers and other architectures across varying model sizes, context lengths, and training paradigms (pretraining and post-training adaptation). In particular, adapting Qwen3.5-4B with MemOnce reduces memory state cache by 1313$\times$ and prefill latency by 6.9$\times$ at 1M context, and improves throughput by 3.5$\times$ and overall memory performance by +5.77%. Finally, MemOnce generalizes zero-shot to downstream representation tasks, achieving competitive results against specialized models.

Long-context models use sparse or structured attention to avoid quadratic cost, yet retrieving a small set of relevant facts embedded among hundreds of thousands of distractors degrades as normalization mass disperses with length. ### [Model Carving: Training Transformers That Optimize Their Own Topology](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27962)
- **Category:** 🌐 Open Models & Data (`open_science`)
- **Authors:** Kaden Zheng, Pratyusha Sharma
- **Signal Score:** `8.6/10.0`
- **Links:** [arXiv Page](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27962) | [Direct PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.27962v1)

**TL;DR:** we introduce Model Carving, which learns both a Transformer's weights and its architecture in a single run.

<details>
<summary><b>Abstract & Metadata</b></summary>

Machine learning has largely kept separate the choice of architecture and the process of training. A classic Transformer, for instance, trains a fixed set of attention and MLP sublayers at every layer, in the same order, and optimizes the weights within. This uniformity is a convention rather than a principled design choice, and it is likely suboptimal: the best architecture for a given task may be smaller, more efficient, and far from uniform. We believe that, ideally, training itself should shape the model's topology. To this end, we introduce Model Carving, which learns both a Transformer's weights and its architecture in a single run. By applying learnable, scalar gates to each sublayer, we allow the training loss to remove the model's own components during training -- freeing a smaller heterogeneous network inside. We evaluate Model Carving by pre-training standard Transformers from 140M to 2.7B parameters and looped Transformers from 140M to 1.3B. At equal training compute, carved models reach a lower validation loss than dense models trained with up to 1.7 times more compute. Furthermore, carved models also outperform size-matched models produced by pruning or distillation, suggesting that the best way to obtain small models for edge devices is to carve them during training, not to compress a large one afterwards. This advantage both grows with scale and carries over to downstream tasks such as few-shot evaluation and generation. Because the removed sublayers no longer run, carved models also cost up to 34% fewer FLOPs per token and decode up to 1.6 times faster without cost in quality. Our results show that architecture, like weights, should be learned and not fixed in advance, and that inside every large transformer is a smaller, faster, stronger one waiting to be carved out.

<summary><b>Abstract & Metadata</b></summary>

Modern generative models are typically studied for their ability to generalize, while memorization is treated as a failure mode. We invert this perspective and ask how a generative model can recall a single datum. We frame memorization as a control problem: a learned conditioning signal steers a frozen diffusion model toward a target image, and we follow the formation of this memory throughout training. Before converging to exact reproduction (verbatim), memory passes through a regime of abstraction: samples preserve the target's semantic content (gist) while remaining diverse in appearance, leaving predictable detail to the generative prior. This semantic organization emerges despite the absence of labels, captions, or any explicit semantic objective. In the abstraction regime, the learned memory approaches ordinary text conditioning and supports editing, one-shot recognition, and retrieval more effectively than in the near-perfect reconstruction regime. These results suggest that memorization can serve as a probe of the relevance structure encoded by a generative model: what must be stored about a datum, what can be reconstructed from prior knowledge, and how learned memories interpolate between reconstructive recall and literal data storage.
